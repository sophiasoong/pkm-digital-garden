**Subtitle:** How to balance the emotional and rational to create powerful palettes

**Author:** Ruxandra Duru, Visual Designer (@ruxandraduru) — co-creator of Color Moods, a
tool for generating intentional color combinations. Works with Google designers on the
intersection of color, emotion, and UX design.

Framing: color stays mysterious and intimidating even for seasoned designers. The author's
answer is a three-step approach — **balancing, relating, and completing** your colors —
bracketed by emotional gut checks at both ends.

## Follow your feeling

Assessment starts with the immediate feeling a scheme generates. Joy, warmth, expansion,
sweetness, calm signal success. Nostalgia, bittersweetness and sadness are called out as
*even greater* indicators and powerful emotions to elicit through color — supported by
Susan Cain's *Bittersweet* on why sad art can move us more than happy work, and by
Christopher Alexander's *The Nature of Order*, whose chief target emotion in the built
environment is sadness.

Irritation, indifference, dislike, or contraction mean: keep adjusting.

## A recalibrated view of color

### Basic color properties

- **Hue** — what we usually mean by "color"; what distinguishes blue from red. Organized on
  a wheel, grouped by temperature, warm on one side and cool on the other.
- **Intensity (chroma)** — how close a color is to its most vibrant, pure state. Distinct
  from **saturation**, which is how much gray a color contains. A fully saturated color can
  have low intensity.
- **Lightness (value/tone)** — how light or dark. Pure hues have *different inherent*
  lightness: yellow reads close to white, purple close to black.

### Seeing the breadth of color

Prompted by Joel Meyerowitz's paired black-and-white and color photographs taken seconds
apart — the color images showed how color "infuses everything," adding a dimension of
information and aliveness.

The central critique: we conceptualize color through an artificial dichotomy.

- One end is "colorful" — meaning joyful color at *maximal intensity*. "What's your favorite
  color?" really asks "what's your favorite vivid color?", which makes color subjective and
  triggering.
- The other end is the supposedly sophisticated and safe non-colorful: black, white, grays,
  neutrals, occasionally browns.

This misses the enormous middle of the intensity spectrum, which has few default names — so
we reach for "terra-cotta," "butter," "sea blue." These are the useful colors, both colorful
and comforting, the ones we evolved surrounded by. In nature, outside pops in fruit, flowers
and some animals, most color is far less vibrant than a typical RGB wheel.

### Understanding color is understanding color relationships

Analyzing colors individually by hue is called intellectually interesting but not useful in
practice. What matters is the whole: the subdued brown lifting a blue and making it glow,
the off-white underneath making both look deeper.

Color is relative. The visual system tries to make neighboring colors *more different*:

- Hues are pushed apart
- Lightness is pushed apart
- Intensity is pushed apart

The effect intensifies when one color surrounds another.

> "There is no such thing as a perfectly neutral backdrop."

Demonstrated with two illusions in which a single continuous strip appears lighter/warmer
or more intense depending only on the background behind each end.

## A three-step approach to creating color combinations

### Step 1: Balance the stimulation

Stimulation ranges from calm → balanced → energizing. Thinking in these terms systematizes
color's subjectivity and avoids apathy, irritation and overwhelm, which attach to under- and
overstimulation. Best to avoid both extremes.

Stimulation rises with:

- Overall **intensity**
- **Hue distance**
- **Light-dark contrast**

These work like sliders on a mixing board — push one up, bring another down. Individually:

- **Reduced intensity via desaturation** → sober, nostalgic, melancholic. Pale saturated →
  joyful, sweet. Dark saturated → hard, strong.
- **Hue distance** — close hues feel gentle, harmonious and rich; distant/complementary hues
  activate and satisfy. More distinct colors in a palette also raises stimulation.
- **Light-dark contrast** — powerful but often forgotten, especially with vibrant colors.
  High contrast gives crisp, energizing, legible results; moderate feels balanced; low
  introduces atmosphere and soothing stillness as colors blend. **Exception:** vibrant
  contrasting hues at the *same* lightness produce vibration, which reads as unpleasantly
  overstimulating — so keep intensity soft or hues close when working at low contrast.

**Atmosphere** is produced by soft light-dark contrast plus gradients and fuzzy boundaries;
even vivid colors feel gentler in that context.

Scale matters too: larger surfaces stimulate more than small ones. A tiny patch of highly
vibrant color, or a whole wall washed in a blush, can each be right. Micro-shifts of color —
on a rock surface, say, or other natural elements — add a subtle stimulation that flat color
lacks.

### Step 2: Relate

Colors work together when they share something — a "glue" that lets the palette read as one,
which is what translates into beauty. Colors from completely different worlds read as
disjointed and discordant.

Relatedness is achieved by restricting one property at a time:

- **Relate via hue** — pick from one chunk of the wheel or one temperature. Neutrals count.
- **Relate via intensity** — joyfully light and saturated, nostalgic and mildly muted, or
  natural, calm and neutral.
- **Relate via lightness** — light, medium, or dark.

Additional approaches: bathe all colors in an imaginary colored light (under warm light,
warm colors stay bright while cool ones desaturate, and vice versa). Or, to keep colors
intact, add a **bridge color** — any color reading as a mixture of the parents, generally
more muted and sitting between the parent hues.

How much relatedness? More shared properties means more harmony but also more predictability.
A dose of variation, even dissonance, adds surprise and stimulation.

### Step 3: Complete

The satisfying kind of variation: colors that complete each other — the complementary
approach and its derivatives. Even a child knows blue balances orange and that both are
emphasized in the process.

Because this is a high-contrast, high-stimulation context, the "vibrant by default" bias is
most urgent here: an orange and blue need not be their most intense versions to deeply
satisfy. And completion is not restricted to hue — dark emphasizes and completes light.

Satisfaction is heightened when **proportions are dramatically unequal**. With a lot of one
thing, we yearn for what counteracts it; a touch of the balancing element brings relief — a
composition flooded with warm yellows balanced by a pinch of cool, or a light scheme
sharpened by a touch of black.

Completion also extends past pairs: with more than two hues, pick them to roughly balance
each other around the wheel. Colorist Donald Kaufman calls this "re-creating light." Split
complementary and triad schemes are described as attempts at the same thing.

## Conclusion

Don't forget emotion. Gerhard Richter would leave a finished painting on the wall and assess
it fresh the next day; if it survived multiple days and multiple gut checks, it was good.
Wherever logic and rules lead, do another gut check and trust the spontaneous emotion.

## Additional color resources (as listed)

- *Interaction of Color*, Josef Albers — "the original color relativity manual"
- *Chromorama*, Riccardo Falcinelli — color across theory, sociology, history, science
- *The Elements of Color*, Johannes Itten — classic in synthesized form; covers hue,
  lightness and intensity contrast
- *Joyful*, Ingrid Fetell Lee — the chapters on pops of bright color; named as the book that
  inspired thinking in terms of stimulation
- Coolors app — useful less for generating palettes than for noting colors seen around you
- "Two-Color Combinations: A Toolkit" — in-depth article on stimulation in two-color
  palettes; **named as the origin of Color Moods**
- colormoods.co — shows stimulation at work in two-color palettes; lock a color and play
