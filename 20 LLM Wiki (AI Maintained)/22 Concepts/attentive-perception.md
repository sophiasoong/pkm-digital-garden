## Definition

The idea that the vast or eternal is found within the small and momentary through close attention, rather than reached by leaving the small thing behind to seek something bigger elsewhere. Per Blake's opening quatrain to "Auguries of Innocence," this is a claim about how you perceive, not about scale: a grain of sand can hold "a world" if attended to fully enough. The vastness isn't hiding somewhere else — it's available in the thing right in front of you, given the right kind of looking.

## How It Appears in This Wiki

Introduced via the opening quatrain of [[23 Sources/blake-auguries-of-innocence-1803]]. The four lines pair four claims — a world in a grain of sand, a heaven in a wild flower, infinity in a hand, eternity in an hour — each collapsing the distance between something small/momentary and something vast/eternal. This is currently the wiki's only source on the concept; the definition above is entirely Blake's framing, drawn from four lines of a much longer poem not otherwise ingested.

## Related Concepts

- [[22 Concepts/mind-body]] — Thematic parallel, not the same claim. Lao Tzu (per [[23 Sources/le-guin-tao-te-ching-1997]]) collapses the split between mind and body — "you are your body." Blake's quatrain collapses a different split — small/vast, momentary/eternal — through attention rather than embodiment. Both refuse a hierarchy that treats the immediate and present as lesser than something bigger held elsewhere. Worth watching whether future sources treat these as the same underlying move or genuinely distinct ideas.

- [[22 Concepts/six-essentials-of-mathematics]] — A thin link, recorded because it inverts this page's move. [[23 Sources/tao-six-essentials-2026]] argues that number is what lets you describe a quantity precisely to someone who never saw the thing, where poetic language blurs and degrades as it passes between people. Blake's claim is that close attention to the small is what reveals the vast. Both are claims about precision and the small; they disagree about which register gets you there. Worth a sentence rather than a page until a source joins them on purpose.

## Open Questions

- Is this a metaphysical claim (the infinite is actually present in the grain of sand) or a claim about a mode of attention (the infinite appears when perception is full enough, regardless of the object)? The quatrain doesn't disambiguate.
- This is the opening of a 132-line poem not otherwise ingested — does the rest of "Auguries of Innocence" develop or complicate this reading?
- Is the parallel to [[22 Concepts/mind-body]] doing real work, or is it a poetic coincidence of structure (both pair a "lesser" thing with a "greater" one and then deny the hierarchy)?
