## Definition

Dualism, in plain terms: splitting yourself into two — a "you" that matters (usually the mind) and a body treated as separate, lesser, or just a tool. Per Le Guin's Chapter 80 commentary on Lao Tzu, there are two versions of this split:

- **Materialistic dualism** — ignore the body, live entirely in your head.
- **Religious dualism** — look down on the body, treat it as something to endure while waiting for a reward in heaven.

Both are called "dangerous and in danger." The alternative isn't picking a winner between mind and body — it's dropping the split altogether: "you are your body."

## Tensions & Debates

Lao Tzu's complaint is that using your body like a tool — even for a good reason, like training your mind — is still treating the body as separate from "you." That raises an honest question about [[22 Concepts/stoicism]]'s practice of voluntary discomfort: cold showers, plain food, hard beds, all done *on purpose* to build mental toughness. Is that the same move Lao Tzu is warning about, just in Roman robes instead of Taoist ones?

Two ways the same cold shower could go:

- **Looks like dualism** — you grit your teeth and push through, using the discomfort purely as a mental workout. The body is just equipment for the mind's goal. You could be anywhere; you're not really *there*.
- **Looks like unity** — you notice the cold, the urge to tense up, your breath. The discomfort pulls your attention *into* your body instead of away from it.

Same action, opposite relationship to it. Nothing ingested so far settles which one Stoic practice actually is — the Stoic sources describe *why* to do it (build endurance, reduce dependency) but not *how present* the practitioner is meant to be while doing it. That's the open question.
