## Definition

The static thinking fallacy is the error of judging a developing technology by its present capability and treating that snapshot as a durable property of the thing. The term is the source's own: the argument that AI cannot reach the hardest problems or build broad theories is named a static-thinking fallacy, on the grounds that today's systems are primitive compared to what they will be in a few months, let alone years [[23 Sources/szegedy-quo-vadis-mathematics-2026]].

The fallacy's characteristic form is a true statement about the present offered as a permanent limit. Its characteristic tell, on the source's account, is retrospective embarrassment: the early aircraft looked primitive and useless, a few people tried them and crashed at the edge of the jungle, which looked ridiculous — and then the same aircraft reached places the skeptics had treated as unreachable. The source extends this to a warning about identity rather than just reasoning: anyone who stakes their sense of themselves on a technology being unable to do some particular task is building on sand, because the description being relied on is of last spring's models.

## How It Appears in This Wiki

As one essay's argumentative engine. Every objection raised against that essay's forecast is routed through this move, which makes the concept load-bearing for [[22 Concepts/ai-in-mathematics]] and worth isolating so the forecast and the reasoning behind it can be assessed separately.

A second source now supplies the response the first one's framing makes hard to see. [[23 Sources/tao-six-essentials-2026]] does not claim current systems will stay as they are; it claims the axis along which they are supposed to improve is the wrong axis. Problems do not lie on a single line from easy to hard, so "the planes will fly higher and longer" is not a prediction about reaching the summits — humans work deep and narrow, machines broad and shallow, and more of the second does not become the first. That is an objection the static thinking charge cannot absorb, because it concedes the rate of change and disputes the direction. Recorded in full on [[22 Concepts/ai-in-mathematics]].

## Why It Is Worth a Page

Because it is domain-general and this wiki will meet it again. The pattern — a capability objection answered by pointing at a rate of change rather than at a capability — appears wherever a technology is improving faster than its critics are updating, which is a description of most of the subject matter this vault is likely to accumulate. The page is written to be linked to from sources that have nothing to do with mathematics.

## Tensions & Debates

**Stated this broadly, it proves too much.** If any current limitation can be dismissed as a description of an earlier model, then no limitation can ever be cited as evidence about anything, and the forecast becomes unfalsifiable in principle. The fallacy as the source defines it has no companion criterion for distinguishing a limitation that will dissolve from one that will not — and such criteria exist in other domains, usually as an account of the mechanism behind the limit. The source supplies none, which converts a real error into a universal rebuttal.

**The mirror error is not named.** There is a symmetric mistake — assuming an observed rate of improvement continues, that early progress on the easy part predicts progress on the hard part, that a curve has no ceiling. The essay commits to a timeline in months and does not acknowledge that the reasoning cuts both ways. A reader who accepted only the static thinking charge would have no vocabulary for over-extrapolation.

**Selected-case reasoning.** The aviation precedent is doing evidential work while presented as illustration. Technologies whose early crudeness predicted continued crudeness are not available as analogies, because nobody tells stories about them — so the reference class the argument draws on is filtered by outcome.

**The companion criterion now has a candidate.** The page's first complaint is that the fallacy comes with no way to tell a dissolving limit from a durable one. The second source offers the shape of one without naming it: a limit is durable when its cause lies outside the system being scaled. Peer digestion and textbook writing are bottlenecks in people and institutions, so faster generation does not touch them. That is a mechanism rather than a timeline, which is exactly what the charge demands and cannot itself supply.

**Genuine, though.** None of the above makes the charge empty. The essay's strongest moment is its observation that the objections were loudest immediately before the capability arrived, and that the people making them did not update when it did. That pattern is real, and this page should not be read as dismissing the concept because its statement is loose.

## Open Questions

- What would a usable criterion look like? Something like: a limit is durable when its cause is identified and not itself a function of scale, data or compute. The source does not attempt this, and this wiki holds nothing that does.
- Does an axis objection count as static thinking? The second source concedes rapid improvement and still denies the conclusion, which suggests the charge only lands against objections phrased as capability ceilings — and most objections are, which may be why it works so well.
- Does the fallacy apply symmetrically to the proponent? Treating the current state as permanently *sufficient* — flags on rocks as a demonstration of general capability — is the same error with the sign flipped.
- How should a wiki record a forecast with a months-scale timeline? This one is dated and checkable, which is unusual and worth exploiting: it should be revisited rather than left standing.
