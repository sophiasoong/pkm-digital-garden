## Definition

The benchmark critique is the argument that a score which successfully ranks performances does not thereby measure the quality the score is named after, and that treating it as though it does corrodes both the thing measured and the people measured. Its force depends on a distinction between domains where ranking is the point and domains where it is a substitution. One source states the test directly: in sports, if runner A runs faster than runner B, A is the better runner, and that is fine — but a higher IQ score does not make someone smarter, and a university ranked fifth is not really better than one ranked seventh, as the next year's reshuffled rankings demonstrate [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]].

The critique's distinctive move is to put four things usually discussed separately into one category: IQ scores, school rankings, AI leaderboards, and the automated checkbox filters that screen job applicants. The claim is that these are the same error at different scales, and the source's word for the habit is toxic.

## The Positive Account

A critique of measurement that offers no alternative is only a complaint, and this one does offer something. Asked what intelligence is, the source declines to define it and says he knows achievement when he sees it: a poem that knocks you off your feet; a theorem solved — even with AI's help — that represents knowledge mankind had never seen before. The criterion is recognition of a particular thing rather than a position in a distribution, and it is deliberately not portable, which is both its honesty and its weakness.

The personal corollary runs: if you have to live up to standards set by someone else, you are not living for yourself. The brutal truth offered is that unless you are LeBron James or a Nobel laureate, you will always find someone who looks better and has done what you cannot, so comparison guarantees loss. What follows is the interview's repeated refrain — give yourself permission to live the life that was meant for you.

## How It Appears in This Wiki

Through one interview, forcefully and repeatedly, from someone who works in AI and who had been paid to construct a benchmark for AI models. The source notes that irony in one place — around hiring filters, where he admits coaching his own children through exactly the boxes he condemns — and does not apply it to the benchmark-writing itself.

The hiring passage is the most concrete: applicants upload a CV to an automated system with checkboxes they must predict in order not to be sieved out in the first round for no good reason. His summary is that everywhere you look, we have adopted a system where we are replaced by numbers. He contrasts it with dropping off a CV and shaking a business owner's hand, against applying to five hundred jobs online — and asks what is human in any of that.

His counter-case in his own practice is a student nobody's filter would have surfaced: a college dropout and touring rock musician who came to mathematics through Ohm's law while repairing his own equipment, and became a graduate student after a three-hour conversation. The argument is not that the filter is inaccurate but that it cannot see the thing worth seeing: if curing cancer were a matter of checking boxes, it would be cured already.

## Tensions & Debates

**It attacks the only instrument available.** This wiki's material on AI and mathematics rests on exactly two kinds of evidence: undocumented claims by interested parties, and benchmark scores. Tao's call for proper scientific benchmarking — the FrontierMath challenge, with secret solutions, as the attempt — is a response to the first problem that the benchmark critique, taken at full strength, would undercut [[23 Sources/tao-six-essentials-2026]]. The sources do not engage each other, and the gap is real: Ono's critique is about measuring *people*, Tao's benchmarks measure *systems*, and nothing in the critique as stated marks that boundary.

**The sports exemption is unexplained.** Granting that running times rank runners while test scores do not rank minds requires a criterion, and the source supplies only the intuition. The obvious candidate — that the measure and the quality are the same thing in sport and different things in cognition — is exactly what a defender of IQ would dispute.

**"I know achievement when I see it" is not a procedure.** It cannot be delegated, audited, or applied at scale, and the alternative it displaces exists partly because unaided human recognition distributes opportunity unevenly. The source's own best example is a student he recognized in a three-hour conversation — one professor, one applicant, which does not generalize to five hundred applications.

**The critic built one.** He was hired to write benchmark problems for large language models, found it hard, and reports that studying the models' reasoning traces is what triggered his crisis. The benchmark did its job: it produced information that changed an expert's mind. That is a point in favor of benchmarks, made by the source, against the source's own thesis, and left unexamined.

## Open Questions

- Does the critique apply to measuring systems as well as people? Everything the source says about leaderboards is about firms competing for crazy scores, which is a claim about incentives, not about validity.
- Is the complaint about measurement or about monoculture? A single ranking used by everyone has different failure modes from measurement as such, and the hiring material reads much more like the former.
- What replaces the filter at scale? The source wants a movement to slow down and evaluate people for their character and experience, and does not cost it.
- He coached his children through the boxes while telling them to stay genuine. Is that hypocrisy, or the only available strategy — and if the latter, is the critique actionable by anyone who is not already tenured?
