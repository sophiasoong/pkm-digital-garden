## Summary

An early Medium piece by Ruxandra Duru, published five years before her Google Design article, exploring a single narrow move: pairing colors that sit roughly a quarter-turn apart on the color wheel. These are neither analogous (too close, too safe) nor complementary (opposite, the textbook choice) but "halfway" — far enough apart to hold tension, close enough to share something. The article is organized as a catalog of nine such pairs, each with a short characterization of the mood it produces.

The piece reads as exploration rather than system. There is no model of why these pairs work, only careful description of what each one feels like, pair by pair: red with purple, orange with magenta, yellow with magenta, yellow with red, green with orange, green with yellow, blue with green, purple with blue-green, blue with magenta. The Munsell wheel is used as the reference rather than the familiar RYB artist's wheel, which shifts which hues count as a quarter-turn apart.

Its most consequential passage is the conclusion, which undercuts the article's own organizing premise: the hue pair matters less than what is done with value and saturation. The same two hues produce substantially different moods depending on lightness and intensity choices. This is the seed of the stimulation model that the 2026 Google article would later formalize as three interacting properties — and that article names Duru's two-color-combination work as the origin of the Color Moods tool, making this piece the documented upstream end of a five-year arc from catalog to framework.

## Key Points

- "Halfway" combinations sit roughly 90° apart on the color wheel — between analogous and complementary.
- Nine pairs are catalogued with mood notes: red/purple, orange/magenta, yellow/magenta, yellow/red, green/orange, green/yellow, blue/green, purple/blue-green, blue/magenta.
- The **Munsell wheel** is the reference, not the RYB artist's wheel; this changes which hues read as a quarter-turn apart.
- **Conclusion, and the article's most important claim:** value and saturation choices alter the mood of a pair more than the choice of hue pair does.
- Method is descriptive and exploratory — pairs are characterized by feel, with no underlying model offered.

## Questions Raised

- Why 90°? The article asserts the interval works without arguing for it. Is there a perceptual basis, or is it a heuristic that happens to avoid both blandness and the complementary cliché?
- Using the Munsell wheel rather than RYB silently changes which pairs qualify. Which wheel the designer uses therefore changes the answer — unaddressed here.
- If value and saturation matter more than the hue pair, as the conclusion concedes, what is left of the halfway idea? Is it a genuine principle or a convenient entry point into the harder variables? The 2026 article effectively answers by dropping hue-pair catalogs in favor of the three-property model.
- The nine pairs' mood characterizations are the author's own perceptions. Are they culturally portable, or do they reflect one designer's associations?
