## Summary

This is a single-paragraph excerpt from Ursula K. Le Guin's rendition of the Tao Te Ching, drawn from her commentary on Chapter 80 ("Freedom"). It is not the chapter's verse translation itself, only Le Guin's prose gloss. (Publication year 1997 is standard bibliographic record for this edition, not stated within the excerpt itself.)

In it, Le Guin frames Lao Tzu as rejecting two opposite ways of splitting the self from the body: the materialistic dualist, who ignores the body and lives entirely in the head, and the religious dualist, who despises the body while living for a reward in heaven. Both, in her reading, are "dangerous and in danger." Lao Tzu's alternative isn't a compromise between the two — it's dropping the split entirely: you are your body, so there's nowhere else to "go." The passage extends this from the personal to the cosmic: heaven and earth are one, and ordinary daily life is itself participation in the Way.

Given the excerpt is a single paragraph out of an 81-chapter book, this is necessarily a thin source — it establishes the core claim clearly but doesn't develop it with further argument or example.

## Key Points

- Lao Tzu (per Le Guin's reading) treats both materialistic dualism (ignore the body, live in the head) and religious dualism (despise the body, live for heaven) as dangerous.
- His alternative is not balance between mind and body, but identity: you are your body.
- The claim scales up cosmologically — heaven and earth are one, and everyday activity (walking your town's streets) is itself the Way of heaven.
