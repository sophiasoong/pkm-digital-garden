## Summary

A filmed interview with Ken Ono, a mathematician who works at Axiom Math and the University of Virginia, structured as nine viewer questions. The frame is career advice for people who feel they are falling behind, but the substance is an account of what AI has done to mathematical research written by someone who both suffered it and builds it. Ono describes being hired roughly a year earlier by Epoch AI to write very difficult problems as a benchmark for large language models — easy money, he assumed — and finding it hard; the models made mistakes, but the reasoning traces were, in his word, frightening. He names the result an identity crisis and reaches for the image of a late-nineteenth-century sharecropper meeting the first combustion-engine tractor.

The interview's most unusual move is self-directed. Ono says the work he was proudest of as a young mathematician — results that depended on years of accumulated technique, one method learned a few years ago combined with another learned last year — now looks to him like something hollow, and possibly automatable all along. He does not conclude that mathematics is automatable; he distinguishes learning techniques from doing discovery, and argues that research is not asking a question and getting an answer but failing at a question repeatedly until the failures reveal a path. On that account AI lowers the burden of discovery rather than replacing it, and students or faculty who refuse the tools will, in some areas, be left behind.

The constructive core is a taxonomy and a bet. Ono separates three things people conflate under "AI": chatbots; machine-learning systems doing superhuman search, for which he cites the protein-folding Nobel; and formalization, translating natural language into verifiable computer code and then having AI inspect that code for vulnerabilities. He places nearly all his hope in the third. His own group is formalizing mathematical economics with a Harvard mathematical economist, and he reports that some foundational theorems were not accurately portrayed or applied — his example is Robert Aumann's "agree to disagree" theorem, whose fiftieth anniversary falls in 2026, and which on formalization turns out not to permit the thing its popular name describes. From this he draws the line that most directly contradicts this wiki's other AI source: 2026 and 2027 should not be a race for more compute but a race for more truth, by means of formalization.

The remaining questions turn on benchmarks and on being measured. Ono refuses the word superintelligence, declining to call a computer super-intelligent for the same reason he would never have thought to call his car super-fast relative to people. He argues that the only reason superintelligence frightens us is that we have staked our identities on thinking skills, and that benchmarks — IQ scores, university rankings that reshuffle yearly, leaderboard scores, the automated checkbox filters that sieve job applicants — do not measure intelligence at all. He is explicit that this comes from someone who works in AI. The interview closes autobiographically: a fifth-grade mathematics contest where he placed third, the silent car ride home, fifty years of reading that as failure, and finding the plaque in his father's closet after his father died in January.

## Key Points

- Ono is a mathematician at Axiom Math and the University of Virginia; the interview was published by the channel EO, which supplies the nine-question structure.
- About a year before the interview he was hired by Epoch AI, with a group of mathematicians, to write very difficult problems as a benchmark for state-of-the-art large language models.
- He found the task hard. The models made mistakes, but studying the reasoning traces was frightening — the right description, he says, is an identity crisis.
- His analogy is the sharecropper meeting the first combustion-engine tractor: not a tool that extends you, a machine that may end your trade.
- He calls it devastating to see models solve problems that were on his own research program.
- What is distinctive about this technological moment, on his account, is that earlier advances lightened physical work; this one takes mental work, which is the stuff of identities.
- He states plainly that a mathematics graduate or current PhD student is deeply worried, and that there is no dancing around it.
- Self-criticism: the work he was proudest of depended on accumulating techniques over years, and if that was all his success rested on, "maybe that was actually automatable." He now finds something hollow in how he had viewed himself.
- But he distinguishes technique from discovery. Research begins with a question you probably cannot answer; by failing you learn about the conjecture, which reveals further questions, which eventually yields a theorem — then you backtrack to the question you originally could not answer. Two steps forward, one back.
- Doing homework problems taught him techniques, not discovery; it taught him how mathematics fits together well enough to participate in discovery later.
- On that model, AI lowers the burden for performing discovery rather than removing the discovery. Computers can check twenty million cases overnight; he frames accepting that as a choice, and says those who stick to traditional ways will in some areas be left behind.
- Three distinct forms of AI, which he says people constantly conflate: (1) chatbots; (2) machine learning doing superhuman search no person would want to do — he cites John Jumper and Demis Hassabis winning the chemistry Nobel for protein folding; (3) formalization.
- Formalization: translate human natural language into computer code that is an exact interpretation of it, then have AI study the code for vulnerabilities — verifiable computer code. He notes that a large and growing share of deployed code is not written by human programmers, and is not perfect.
- Formalizing a subject reveals that the original framing was incomplete. His group works with a Harvard mathematical economist on formalizing mathematical theories in economics, and found some foundational theorems were not accurately portrayed, implemented or applied.
- Worked example: 2026 is the fiftieth anniversary of Robert Aumann's theorem on whether parties with the same priors can agree to disagree. Aumann's theorem does not allow it — what actually happens is that the parties come to understand each other's perspectives. The subtleties live in the hypotheses, in particular what having the same priors means, and that is where formalization came in. He describes the result as a viral moment in mathematical economics, with economists joining the effort.
- He reports the same effort extending to computer scientists formalizing machine learning itself.
- The job forecast follows: cybersecurity needing legions of computer scientists, plus ethicists and lawyers; formalization specialists building the guardrails. Anything controlled by a mathematical language after translation should be formalized.
- His image for a large language model: the most incredible librarian, one who has read everything — but you do not want your librarian to be your neurosurgeon. High-stakes situations need taste, human judgment, and the emotional intelligence to understand how decisions land on people.
- Advice to students entering college or graduate school who want to be mathematicians: start formalizing. You may still prove unsolved conjectures along the way.
- The direct counter to the compute framing: this is 2026, 2027, and he does not believe now is the race for more compute.
- On judgment: formal verification is a yes-no binary, not judgment. Judgment is how people choose to act on information. He contrasts a drone measuring air quality over Los Angeles with the decision whether a building holds a dangerous person or is a school or hospital, and says it is usually not hard to tell when you want a human in the room.
- He refuses "superintelligence" as a frame — he would never have thought to call his automobile super-fast compared to people. He characterizes the present and near future as AI as co-pilot, tools we cohabitate with.
- The benchmark critique, which recurs throughout: sports rankings are fine, but a higher IQ score does not mean smarter; a university ranked fifth is not really better than one ranked seventh, as next year's reshuffle shows; AI firms competing for leaderboard scores is not intelligence. What is intelligence: a poem that knocks you off your feet, or a theorem solved even with AI's help that represents knowledge mankind had never seen.
- On hiring filters: applicants submit to automated systems with checkboxes they must predict to avoid being sieved out in the first round for no good reason. "Everywhere you look, we have adopted a system where we are replaced by numbers." He says this as someone who works in AI, and admits he coached his own children through exactly those boxes while telling them to stay genuine.
- If you buy into the checkbox system entirely you forget that character and judgment matter: if curing cancer were a matter of checking boxes, it would be cured already.
- His counter-example in talent spotting: Robert Schneider, an independent rock musician (producer for Neutral Milk Hotel, lead singer of Apples in Stereo) and college dropout who started learning electronics to repair touring equipment, encountered Ohm's law, saw his own music-making in it, went back for a mathematics degree, and became Ono's graduate student after a three-hour conversation. Ono reports 35 PhD students in total.
- On a named AI result: he cites OpenAI announcing, a few weeks before the interview, a proof of the Erdős unit distance conjecture — a problem he thought would never be solved in his lifetime. He stresses the post hoc reading: it took human collaboration with the system, and he does not think people alone could have done it without an unlikely assembly of experts from different fields.
- What AI offers, on that reading, is tireless access to accumulated human knowledge, lowering the bar for problems that would otherwise require getting the right people in one room.
- His limit: he doubts AI generates genuinely new ideas. It can compute more and find patterns humans cannot, but he suspects the ideas are already there. His test case is Picasso — good luck finding evidence of that artwork before Picasso — and he says plainly that he does not know.
- On preferring AI company to human company: he works with models daily and his wife teases him about having a relationship with them; he grants she is not wrong, and finds it satisfying when models start thinking like he does. His counsel is still to put it down and go for a walk. He recounts counting people walking through Gangnam looking at their phones and estimating 70%.
- The closing lesson, repeated twice in the interview: give yourself permission to live the life that was meant for you. The brutal truth he offers is that unless you are LeBron James or a Nobel laureate you will always find someone who looks better, so comparison guarantees loss.
- Biography as argument: fifth-grade mathematics contest, third place, famous mathematician father in the audience, silent drive home, fifty years of reading it as failure — and the plaque kept in his father's closet, found when his father died in January. He concludes he had misinterpreted the event his whole life.
- He mentions being a Japanese kid in a very white suburb of Baltimore at a time when that was not good, and drawing strength from it after about ten years.

## Questions Raised

- Ono's three-form taxonomy does real work, but is formalization a form of AI or an application of it? He treats it as a category alongside chatbots and search, which may be a category error that happens to be useful.
- The Aumann example is the only concrete evidence offered that formalization revises a field's foundations. One example, reported by a participant, about his own group's work.
- If formalization is where the jobs are, who formalizes? It requires both domain mastery and proof-assistant fluency, which is a narrower population than either field alone, and the interview does not address the training pipeline it is recommending students enter.
- He doubts AI produces genuinely new ideas, and says he does not know. What would settle it? The Picasso test is unfalsifiable as stated.
- He attacks benchmarks while having been paid to build one. The interview notes the irony around the hiring-filter point but never turns it on the Epoch AI work itself.
- His verdict that job losses will not be as significant as feared sits oddly beside his own account of a trade being ended. He hopes it remains true, which is not an argument.
- The identity-crisis account and the advice are in tension: the crisis came precisely from comparison with a machine's output, which is the thing the closing lesson tells viewers not to do.
