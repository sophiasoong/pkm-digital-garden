## Summary

This is the opening quatrain of Blake's "Auguries of Innocence," a much longer poem (132 lines in its full form, not otherwise ingested here). Blake is believed to have written it around 1803; it wasn't published until 1863, two years after his death, in Alexander Gilchrist's biography of him — so the 1803 date above marks composition, not publication.

The four lines pair something vast or eternal with something small or momentary: a world with a grain of sand, heaven with a wild flower, infinity with the palm of a hand, eternity with an hour. The claim isn't that these small things are secretly large in some literal sense — it's about a mode of perceiving. Look closely and fully enough at something small and ordinary, and it stops being merely small; the vastness was never elsewhere to begin with, waiting to be reached. That reading foregrounds attention itself — how you look — over what you happen to be looking at.

Because only the opening quatrain was staged for ingestion, this source is deliberately narrow: the full poem is a long list of aphorisms on largely unrelated moral and cosmic themes, and this excerpt should not be read as representative of the whole.

## Key Points

- Four parallel claims, each pairing something vast/eternal with something small/momentary: world/grain of sand, heaven/wild flower, infinity/hand, eternity/hour.
- The vastness is found within the small thing through close attention, not reached by leaving the small thing behind.
- This is the opening of a 132-line poem; the excerpt covers only these four lines.
