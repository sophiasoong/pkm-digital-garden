## Summary

A personal essay arguing that pure mathematics is about to be industrialized by AI, and that the loss mathematicians will feel is real but is a loss of status structure rather than of the field itself. The subtitle poses the question the piece answers: is mathematics over, or just graduating? Szegedy's answer is graduating, and his register is deliberately double — he is both a product of competition mathematics and a participant in the AI effort displacing it.

The argument runs on two sustained analogies. The first models pure mathematics as exploration of an endless, unmapped and lethal wilderness, where explorers map terrain, prospect for fundable resources, and hand-build their own gadgets. What that community actually rewards is ranked: most reverence for whoever climbs a tall, previously unclimbed rock and sketches a rough map from the summit, less for connecting two distant regions, less again for a widely used tool, and least for finding a mineral deposit that benefits non-explorers. The admired thing is explicitly not the output but the heroic effort behind it, and the conjectures themselves are described as mostly uninteresting in their own right — testing grounds for new climbing tools, occasionally turning out to be mountains from which the terrain opens up.

The second analogy puts AI where the Wright brothers' twelve-second flight sits in aviation history: primitive, obscenely expensive, narrow, and improving fast. Szegedy claims the planes have this year reached the range and reliability to enter the terrain without crashing and have landed on rocks nobody had climbed. He reports the explorers' reaction as a mixture of irritation at flag-planting on rocks they were mid-ascent of, insistence that the rocks were not that interesting anyway, a complaint that flags deter other climbers, and a request for planes of their own. He calls the objection that planes cannot reach the real summits a static-thinking fallacy, and forecasts cheap charter flights, then satellites, then systematic resource extraction — messy, commercial, and appalling to the people who loved the jungle.

The closing section is the one that does the emotional work, and it is autobiographical. Szegedy opens the essay with the two freest hours of his life, immediately after his high school finals, followed by days of sadness at realizing the competitions were over: nobody coached him anymore, nobody cared, and he did not know who he was without something to train for. He predicts many mathematicians will feel exactly that — disorientation and sudden loss of purpose — and says the grief is real and will not last. What changes, on his account, is not what mathematics produces but what it rewards and whom it respects: choosing which terrain is worth exploring, steering fleets of aircraft, and explaining the results outward replace the specific heroism of climbing what nobody else could.

## Key Points

- The thesis is about the reward structure of a field, not its output. Mathematics is forecast to become larger, more useful and more mundane at once, while the heroism it currently venerates is devalued from outside.
- Pure mathematics is modeled as exploration: mapping terrain (building theories), climbing rocks (settling conjectures), building gadgets (tools and methods), and finding mineral deposits (applications).
- The internal status ranking is stated explicitly, highest to lowest: heroic solo ascent of a hard, previously unclimbed rock; a river connecting two distant regions; a widely used tool; a commercially valuable deposit.
- Tools are a second-class achievement and are not believed useful until used to climb something nobody has climbed — so tool-building is validated through rock-climbing rather than on its own terms.
- Funding is framed as instrumental and slightly cynical: explorers hardly care about financial rewards for their own sake, but applications get mentioned in grant proposals because "financiers" who believe more deposits lie nearby will pay for expeditions.
- Conjectures are described as usually uninteresting in themselves — testing grounds for methods. Occasionally one turns out to be a mountain, and that rare case is why people stay.
- AI's position is dated: until this year the systems flew a few hundred metres at a few metres' altitude; in the last few months they reached range and reliability sufficient to enter the terrain, and "a few conjectures" were solved.
- No conjecture, system, lab or result is named anywhere in the essay. The central empirical claim is asserted without citation.
- The AI side's flag-planting on in-progress problems is conceded to be PR, and the complaint that it deters other climbers is reported without rebuttal.
- Honing artisan tools by climbing rocks is forecast to lose its point once anyone who can afford a charter can land on any rock.
- Cheap flight is forecast to be followed by satellites: whole-terrain mapping, then extraction — messy, dirty, commercial, and in the author's words something today's explorers will be right to resent.
- The objection that AI cannot conjecture, theorize or explain is dismissed as a description of last spring's models, and staking an identity on "AI can't do task X" is called building on sand.
- Academia is predicted to change slowly in form — universities, journals and tenure committees looking much the same for years — while what they reward shifts fundamentally.
- Mathematics is named the most meritocratic academic field, which the essay treats as the reason the shift will go through: merit will simply be redefined and the field will follow.
- The forecast endpoint is mathematics as full-scale industry and the infrastructure every other field stands on, rather than a quiet corner of academia.
- The author's own position is not neutral and the text does not hide it: he uses "we" for the side that solved the conjectures and planted the flags, and closes anticipating the view from his own supersonic airplane.

## Questions Raised

- Which conjectures were solved, by which systems? The essay's load-bearing empirical claim is unsourced, and this is the first thing a second source should settle.
- If a field's status economy is separable from its output, what happens to the output when the incentive to produce it is removed? The essay assumes mathematics keeps being done, in volume, by people differently motivated.
- Is the static-thinking charge falsifiable? If every current limitation can be dismissed as a description of last spring's models, no limitation can ever be cited as evidence. The essay offers no criterion for telling a temporary limit from a durable one.
- The grief is predicted to pass. On what basis? The author's own case — adolescent competition loss followed by a successful career on the other side of the change — is a single data point, and an unusually fortunate one.
- Does the meritocracy argument hold, or invert? The essay treats mathematics being meritocratic as the reason the transition goes through smoothly. A field that distributes status by demonstrated individual difficulty is arguably the one with most to lose when difficulty is purchasable.
- Who owns the airplanes? The essay forecasts large companies finding unimaginable riches in the terrain and treats this as regrettable but minor. It does not ask what it means for a public science to depend on privately held instruments.
