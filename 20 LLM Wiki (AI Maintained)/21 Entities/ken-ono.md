## Overview

Mathematician, speaker in [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]. He works at [[21 Entities/axiom-math]] and the University of Virginia, and the interview's interest for this wiki is that he occupies the same structurally awkward position as [[21 Entities/christian-szegedy]]: a mathematician employed on the AI side of a transition he describes as devastating to his own trade. Everything here comes from that interview, per this wiki's practice for figures known through a single source — though unusually, he supplies a good deal of autobiography himself, so the page is less thin than that practice usually produces.

What makes him more than a second instance of Szegedy is that his conclusions run the other way. Where Szegedy's essay argues from trajectory toward abundant automated problem-solving, Ono names compute as the wrong axis and formalization as the right one, and spends much of the interview attacking the measurement culture — benchmarks, rankings, automated filters — that Szegedy's account of mathematical status describes without objecting to. He is also notably willing to turn the criticism inward: he says the achievements he was proudest of may have been automatable all along, and finds something hollow in how he had understood himself as a mathematician.

## Key Facts

- Works at Axiom Math and the University of Virginia. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- Fifty-eight years old at the time of the interview, and frames several answers around wanting to see certain questions answered in his lifetime. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- About a year before the interview, was part of a group of mathematicians hired by Epoch AI to write very difficult problems as a benchmark for state-of-the-art large language models. He expected easy money and found the task hard. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- Describes studying the models' reasoning traces as frightening, and the resulting experience as an identity crisis; calls it devastating to see models solve problems that were on his own research program. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- Has supervised 35 PhD students. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- Names Robert Schneider as the most colorful case: an independent rock musician and college dropout who came to mathematics through Ohm's law while repairing touring equipment, and became his graduate student after a three-hour first conversation. Ono uses him as evidence against checkbox admissions. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- His group at Axiom is formalizing mathematical theories in economics with a Harvard mathematical economist, and reports finding that some foundational theorems were not accurately portrayed, implemented or applied — his example being Robert Aumann's theorem on agreeing to disagree. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- Reports the effort extending to computer scientists formalizing machine learning itself. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- Advises students who want to be mathematicians to start formalizing now. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- Refuses the term superintelligence, saying he would never have thought to call his car super-fast relative to people, and describes the present as AI as co-pilot. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- Biography he offers himself: a Japanese kid in a very white suburb of Baltimore at a time when that was not good to be; a fifth-grade mathematics contest where he placed third in front of his father, a famous mathematician, followed by a silent drive home and fifty years of reading it as failure; and finding that plaque in his father's closet after his father died in January. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]
- Works with AI models daily and grants his wife's teasing that he has a relationship with them, while still advising viewers who find themselves talking to chatbots as if to people to put the phone down and go for a walk. [[23 Sources/ono-nine-lessons-2026]]

## Contradictions

*(None between sources on matters of fact — the two other sources on AI and mathematics do not discuss him.)*

Two internal tensions worth recording, since both bear on how his claims should be weighed:

- He is severe about benchmarks as a measure of people, having been paid to write one for machines. The interview notices the irony when discussing hiring filters but never turns it on the Epoch AI work.
- He says the loss of jobs will not be anywhere near as significant as feared, and hopes that remains true — immediately after describing a trade being ended, with a sharecropper analogy chosen precisely because the sharecropper's trade did end.
